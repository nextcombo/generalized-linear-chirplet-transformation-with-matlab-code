# Generalized Linear Chirplet Transformation [with MATLAB Code]

Introduction: Signal processing technology, as an important tool for understanding the physical world, has attracted widespread attention over a considerable period. The classic Fourier transform (FT) is a standard tool that reveals the overall frequency content by assuming the considered signal is stationary. However, the assumption of stationarity is not always met in practice, and the concept of non-stationarity is closer to reality. Examples include vibration responses of rotating machinery under non-stationary events (acceleration, deceleration, or even fault impacts), bat echolocation signals, human speech signals, etc. For non-stationary situations, the total frequency obtained from FT is insufficient to describe these signals, making the concept of instantaneous frequency (IF) more suitable for characterizing time-varying features. The famous definition of IF was proposed by Villé, which describes the local oscillation speed at a certain moment of the signal [1]. The mid-frequency of a signal can be calculated by its derivative of the phase of its analytical signal, but it only applies to single-component signals. Fortunately, time-frequency analysis (TFA) provides an alternative and powerful solution to characterize the IF features of non-stationary signals [2,3]. This is because the energy distribution of the time-frequency representation generated by TFA is concentrated around the mid-frequency of the analyzed signal on the time-frequency plane. Then, the IF of the considered signal can be estimated from the well-established TFA representation. Therefore, to precisely estimate the mid-frequency characteristics, the key is that the TFA method can obtain higher TFA resolution. There are many types of TFA methods, which can generally be divided into two categories: linear TFA and quadratic TFA. In classical linear Fourier analysis, such as Short Time Fourier Transform (STFT) and Wavelet Transform (WT), the characteristic of the signal lies in the inner product of the TF-positionable atomic dictionary. Therefore, the TF resolution of linear TFA mainly depends on the TF atoms. According to the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, linear TFA cannot achieve arbitrary high TF resolution simultaneously. This means that fine frequency resolution leads to coarse time resolution, and vice versa. Traditional linear TFA is based on the assumption that the considered signal is segmented in short time periods as being stationary. This means that they essentially use horizontal lines to approximate IF features. However, for emphasis on modulated frequency signals, the IF of the signal is also time-varying in short windows, which will lead to poor TF resolution [4,5]. In classical quadratic TFA, such as Wigner-Ville Distribution (WVD), it is the Fourier transform of the instantaneous autocorrelation function of the signal. For single-component signals, the WVD method can obtain high TF resolution TF representations, but when dealing with multi-component signals, it inevitably introduces cross terms.
From the analysis above, it is evident that traditional TFA methods are insufficient to provide high-resolution TF representations due to their inherent limitations. To enhance the performance of TFA methods, many efforts have been made over the years, leading to the development of numerous effective approaches. These methods can generally be categorized into two strategies: TF redistribution and parameterized TFA.
The inspiration for parameterized TF analysis methods comes from the observation that STFT and CWT employ different analysis windows (e.g., different widths and chirp rates) to achieve varying TF resolutions. Selecting appropriate window functions allows for alignment with the inherent characteristics of the signal under consideration, resulting in a higher TF resolution for the obtained TF representation. It can be seen that the early parameterized TF A method uses segmented linear windows to approximate time-varying TF features. However, when encountering strong nonlinear signals, it is not a good choice. In [14], Wang proposed a method called Matched Demodulation Transform (MDT). The MDT method utilizes time-varying demodulation operators to describe the nonlinear characteristics of the intermediate frequency, which can significantly improve the energy concentration of TF representations. Parallelly, by introducing rotational and translational operators from [22], it allows the construction of nonlinear window functions. If a nonlinear model consistent with the signal is used, then TF representations with better concentrated energy can be generated. Peng et al. have proposed several nonlinear mathematical models to describe the intrinsic features of various types of analytical signals, such as polynomial models [22], spline models [23], and generalized wavelet models [24]. They unified these methods under the generalized parameterized TF A (GPTFA) [25]. Although GPTFA shows strong TF resolution for analyzing strong nonlinear intermediate frequency signals, its limitations should not be overlooked. Due to the complexity and diversity of real-world situations, we cannot use a single mathematical model to describe all types of measurement signals. Moreover, a well-established model is only applicable to specified single components in the signal, so it cannot handle multicomponent signals with different intermediate frequency characteristics. Additionally, parameterized models need to be estimated from traditional TFAs, thus requiring an initial TFA with fair energy concentration, otherwise an accurate model cannot be estimated. Under these constraints, there is still much room for improvement in GPTFA.
SST and GPTFA, as TFA techniques developed in recent years, can analyze signals more accurately, but their inherent limitations should not be overlooked. Considering their shortcomings, improvements and developments in TFA methods should focus on the following capabilities: (1) effectively characterize multicomponent signals with obvious nonlinear characteristics; (2) independent of mathematical models and initial TFA methods; (3) allow reconstruction of interested components; (4) be as noise-insensitive as possible.
